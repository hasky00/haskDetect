The BTCPay Server hot wallet sweep: 10,471 merchant addresses drained into one Taproot wallet

An on-chain reconstruction of the November 2026 BTCPay Server incident, built from public Bitcoin node data via mempool.space. Every figure below comes from the transactions themselves and can be re-pulled with the script in tracer/.

Open — sweeps still landing at time of writing Written 2 Nov 2026, 10:00 CET Chain: Bitcoin mainnet Source: mempool.space public API, no proprietary labels

What happened, in one paragraph

On 1 November 2026 at 22:25 UTC, BTCPay Server told every operator to shut their instance down: hot wallets were being swept. The attacker's collection address, published by founder Nicolas Dorier, is bc1p0f7wmcnesrapznymf9ljdn04pudgmh96sqtrm9vmgqxdh666fh8qvn63r7. The chain shows it received its first stolen coins on 30 October at 13:35 UTC, roughly 57 hours before the alert. Between then and the alert, 321 sweep transactions moved 16.73 BTC from 10,471 distinct merchant addresses into it. Only 1.73 BTC arrived after the alert. The stolen coins are being cashed out in round lots (5.5, 4.71, 1.3, 0.67, 0.65 BTC) to fresh single-use bc1q addresses, none of which has moved on yet. The address is still active: the last confirmed sweep was 07:35 UTC on 2 November and another is sitting in the mempool as this is written.

Open. Dorier's screenshot (about 00:30 UTC, 2 Nov) showed 18.75 BTC received and 522 transactions. Nine hours later the address shows 19.35 BTC received and 587 transactions. Instances that are still up and still unpatched are still being emptied. The victims' entry point (how admin access was obtained on pre-2.4.2 instances) is not visible on-chain and is not claimed here; BTCPay says it is investigating.

How it worked, from the chain's point of view

A BTCPay hot wallet is a seed on a web server

BTCPay's hot-wallet option stores the signing seed on the same internet-facing machine that runs the checkout. Whoever is a server administrator can read it, or simply use the built-in Send page. BTCPay's own founder has said the attacker logged in as admin on instances older than 2.4.2; how is still under investigation. On-chain we cannot see logins. What we can see is the result: hundreds of unrelated small wallets, each sending its entire balance to the same brand-new Taproot address, at 1 to 2 sat/vB.

Two sweep styles, one hand

305 of the 568 sweeps have a single input: one UTXO, the whole wallet, one output to the collector. The rest consolidate many UTXOs from one victim at once, up to 1,316 inputs in a single transaction. Both styles pay almost nothing in fees (median 2.1 sat/vB; the three giant batches at 16:50 paid 1.0) and 464 of them signal RBF. Low fee, RBF on, hundreds of victims, two days of patience: the attacker was in no hurry and expected no one to notice. They were right for 57 hours.

Why 10,471 addresses is not 10,471 victims

A BTCPay store generates a fresh receive address for every invoice, so one merchant's hot wallet can hold hundreds of UTXOs on hundreds of addresses. The 568 sweep transactions are the better proxy for the number of victim wallets, and even that over-counts stores that were swept more than once. The right order of magnitude is "a few hundred merchants", not ten thousand.

What this is not. Not a Bitcoin protocol flaw, not a Lightning channel drain (that was the August 2026 BTCPay incident, fixed in 2.4.2), not a phishing campaign against users. The chain shows legitimately-signed transactions from wallets whose keys were readable by an attacker with admin access to the server holding them. Who that attacker is, and whether they reused access gained during the August window, is not visible here.

Seventeen of nineteen outgoing payments went to an address that had never been used before and has not moved since. Round amounts to fresh single-use addresses is the shape of exchange deposits: each lot is sized for a deposit, parked, and the attacker waits to see which ones get frozen. The two that did move (30 Oct) were the small early tests. The full list with balances is in evidence/destination_addresses.json; those eighteen addresses are the ones for exchanges to flag.

What a detector would have seen

HaskDetect scores behaviour, not labels. Reading only public mempool data, these signals were available from the first afternoon:

01
Fan-in to a newborn addressA Taproot address with zero history receives from 2, then 21, then 15 unrelated wallets in one day. Many-to-one from strangers into a fresh address is the opposite of normal merchant flow.
02
Full-balance sends from small wallets305 single-input, single-output sweeps: the whole UTXO, no change. A merchant till does not empty itself, with no change output, to a stranger.
03
Send-then-test within ten minutesFirst sweep 13:35, first cash-out 13:45, and that cash-out was spent on again. Confirming the exit works before scaling is textbook rehearsal, the Bitcoin twin of Case 001's three EIP-7702 dress rehearsals.
04
Patient fees, RBF onMedian 2.1 sat/vB with replace-by-fee signalled on 464 of 568. The attacker optimised for cost over speed because nothing was chasing them. Theft at the minimum fee rate is a behavioural tell on its own.
05
Industrial consolidationThree 566-input transactions in the same block, then a 5.5 BTC round lot out sixteen minutes later. Scripted collection followed by a timed cash-out.
06
Round-lot exits to virgin addresses5.5, 4.71, 1.3, 1.3, 0.67, 0.65 BTC, each to an address with no past and no future. Deposit-sized, parked, waiting to see what gets frozen.

Signal 01 fired 57 hours before the public alert. A watcher on the BTCPay side needs none of this: any hot wallet whose entire balance leaves to an address that hundreds of other BTCPay wallets are also paying is compromised, and that check is one API call per outgoing transaction.
